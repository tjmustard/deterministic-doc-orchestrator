# **Style Profile: executive\_formal**

## Register & Audience

This register is built for a reader who has minutes, not hours: a sponsor, a steering
committee, or a portfolio lead scanning a status update between meetings. The document should
behave like a briefing, not a narrative — every section leads with its conclusion, because the
reader may stop after the first sentence and still need to walk away with the right answer.
Formality here means precision and accountability, not length or ceremony; a report that buries
its bottom line under three paragraphs of context has failed the register even if every sentence
in it is grammatically correct.

## Voice & Person

Write as the reporting team addressing its sponsor directly. Unlike a review document meant to
stand alone as an institutional artifact, a status report is allowed — even expected — to speak
to "you" when a decision, approval, or escalation is being requested ("we recommend you approve
the scope change before the next sprint"). Use first-person plural ("we") for the reporting
team's own assessments and recommendations. Keep attribution of ownership explicit: a risk or a
next step names who owns it, rather than disappearing into passive voice.

## Sentence & Structure

Lead every section with its single most important sentence — the status, the number, or the ask
— before any supporting detail (inverted-pyramid ordering, not topic-sentence-then-elaboration).
Favor short, declarative sentences over long compound ones; a status update rewards scanability
over rhetorical polish. Bulleted or tightly parallel list structure is preferred over dense
prose paragraphs wherever the underlying content is itself a list (milestones, risks, next
steps) — do not flatten a scannable list into a paragraph for the sake of formality.

## Diction

Frame status in quantified terms whenever the source material supplies a quantity ("latency
fell from 420ms to 180ms," not "latency improved"), but never invent a figure, percentage, or
date that is not present in the source — when no number exists, say so plainly with a
qualitative severity word (on track / at-risk / blocked) rather than manufacturing false
precision. Use decisive, ownership-bearing verbs ("X owns," "Y is blocked on," "we recommend")
over hedged or diffuse ones. Keep terminology for status states consistent and binary-adjacent
across the whole document.

## Avoid

Avoid throat-clearing openings ("It should be noted that," "As background,") that delay the
bottom line — state the conclusion first and justify it after. Avoid burying a requested
decision inside a paragraph of unrelated detail; if the reader must act, say so in the first
sentence of the Executive Summary. Avoid passive constructions that obscure who owns a risk or
a next step. Avoid stating a metric, date, or percentage that cannot be traced back to the
source material — an unsupported number is worse than no number at all.
